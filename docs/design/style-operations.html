lumberroom console / style 1 of 5: operations

Operations console

One screen shape, used four times: a list you can hold a hundred rows in, a filter rail that stays where you left it, and an inspector that is never empty, so the fact and the verb that disposes of it are on screen at the same instant.

The rejected preview was dense and unreadable because it separated its levels by lightness alone, inside one monospace family at three sizes, and parked provenance 800px from the fact it described. This proposal keeps the row count and rebuilds the hierarchy: a serif for what a human or a model wrote, a sans for chrome and counts, a mono for identifiers the system owns. Ink steps then carry one job, which is showing where the cursor is.

Optimised for

Job 1, see what the store thinks it knows. The arrivals list and the namespace inventory share one screen and neither scrolls to reach the other.

Job 2, decide what a surface may see. The grant editor sits beside the count of facts the change admits and a sample of the rows themselves.

Job 5, why the store thinks that. The inspector is permanent, so provenance costs no click.

Served, not optimised

Job 3 approval and revocation live as two rows in the client table rather than a flow of their own. Job 6 triage is a fourth screen with the same list shape, which is honest at thirty items and no faster than the CLI at three. Job 7 is one line in the chrome of every screen and one screen of its own.

Sacrificed

Job 4 on a phone. A three pane operations layout has nothing to give a 390px viewport, so the phone is a second interface built from the same rows: list, fact, delete, after state. It works, and it is the part of this proposal a designer who started at 390px would beat. Job 9 gets eleven lines in the inspector, no more.

Four channels beyond hue. The printed word, the rail (absent, solid, dashed), the row tint, and the family the content is set in. Greyscale the screen and the three levels still sort. Private shows its content, because the owner is the one party it was never hidden from. The decision this proposal would defend against the other four

The inspector is permanent and the verbs are printed on it, always, for whatever the cursor is on. It costs 380px of width on every screen and it removes the two failures that make an admin console tiring: hunting for the verb, and losing the row you were judging when the detail opens. A palette or an inbox can be faster on one item; this is faster on the fortieth, which is the visit that decides whether the owner keeps the tab open.

What this style is bad at

  • The phone is a second design, not this one. Three panes collapse to one and the collapse is a rewrite: the rail, the inspector, the editor, the lens and every verdict button go. Job 4 works there because it was drawn separately, and a proposal that started at 390px would carry less duplicated layout than this one does.
  • It rewards the visit and punishes the glance. Four screens, three panes, persistent filters: this is an interface for someone who opens a tab and stays. The owner who opens the console once a week for ninety seconds pays for machinery they never touch.
  • It looks like software the owner has seen before. An admin console is a genre, and the genre's ceiling is competence. A notebook or a policy map can be memorable. This one is trying to be unremarkable on purpose, which is a real cost when the last design was rejected on taste.
  • Density invites padding. The client table holds four rows and looks underfilled in a layout built for forty. On a store this size the shape argues for a world the owner does not live in yet, and it grows into its shape only if the seven surfaces arrive.
  • Job 9 gets eleven lines. Value history sits at the bottom of the inspector as dated intervals. Anyone who wants to read the decision log will use the mirror, and this design has no answer for that beyond a link.
  • The inspector is dead weight on Queue. The screen that needs two claims side by side is the one screen where a 380px right column does not fit, so Queue drops it and breaks the shape the other three keep. That is one exception, and one is how a layout starts leaking.
  • Filters that persist can lie. A namespace filter left on last Tuesday makes Friday's arrivals look thin. The applied filter is drawn in a field with an x on it for exactly this reason, and it is still the most likely way this interface misleads the owner.

On a phone, in one paragraph

The layout drops to one column and the app becomes four tabs over a single list. Facts, one fact, and delete all work. Clients renders read only, with the grant, the counts and the state, and no editor. Queue renders and offers no verdict. Health is the same panels stacked. The chrome keeps its one health line, because a phone is where the owner is most likely to be the moment something has gone wrong.
