lumberroom console · triage inbox

The queue is the store, and the unit is the arrival.

Everything in this console is an item to dispose of, and reading an item disposes of it. The inbox does not wait for a conflict to have something to show: every fact that lands in the store is an item, sorted newest first, with the four kinds that need a verdict pinned above them. Namespaces are the folders. One column holds the queue, one holds the item, large.

Ranked jobs I optimised for

  • Job 1, see what the store thinks it knows. The arrivals rail is the landing screen and the namespace folders are the inventory. Both sit above the fold, side by side, because they are the same screen.
  • Job 6, triage. The style's home ground. Four kinds sort above the arrivals with two or three verbs each and a keyboard path down the whole queue.
  • Job 5, why the store thinks that. Opening any item is the provenance panel. Job 1 and job 5 are one interaction here.
  • Job 4, get one thing out, now. The phone runs the same queue, read-only for verdicts, with delete kept end to end.

What I sacrificed

  • Job 2, grant composition. A client sits in the queue well as a decision. Editing seven clauses does not, and I gave it a full-width panel that stops being an inbox for as long as it is open. A policy map beats me here.
  • Job 8, the lens, is a control inside that panel and nothing more.
  • Job 9, what I used to believe, is four lines in the item panel. The spec ranks it last and I spent nothing on it.

The arrival rate problem, answered rather than dodged

Nobody has counted how many conflicts appear in a week, and §9 Q6 leaves it open. An inbox whose only content is conflicts is an empty screen most Mondays, which is a bad landing page and a worse habit.

So arrivals are items. The store is written every working day, so the queue always has something true in it, and the count that matters stays honest: the badge counts what needs a verdict, and it reads 0 when nothing does.

Arrivals never demand a verdict. Reading is the disposal. One key marks the rail caught up and moves a watermark, and no arrival ever asks the owner to classify, confirm or file anything. PRD §8 names the failure this avoids: everything ends in open because permissions became a chore. An inbox that asked for a verdict on 200 facts a week would invent the same chore in a new place.

Two densities, one screen

An item that needs a decision gets three lines and a subject in 17px serif. An arrival gets one line at 15px with its provenance welded to the end of the sentence. That is the difference between the two, and it is the whole grammar of the rail.

What killed the last one, and what I did instead

Defect in the rejected previewWhat this design does
One family, three sizes, five greys. Every operating surface sat in an 11 to 13px mono band. Three families with separate work. Serif carries the fact and nothing else, sans carries verbs and provenance, mono carries identifiers only: namespaces, keys, ids, byte counts, commands. Facts run 15px in the rail and 26px open.
Provenance 800px from its fact, in a 27ch column that wrapped. Provenance follows the fact in the same line and shrinks the fact before it moves. In the open item it is a sentence under the claim, never a column.
Annotation outweighed content. Dotted chips matched the verdicts beside them. Not one status chip inside a frame. The pins that mark missing server work live in this document, in the margin outside the screen, where the owner reads and never operates.
A configuration warning was the loudest thing on the triage screen. The key state is one line in the top bar of every screen. When it breaks it becomes the first item in the queue, which is the one full treatment, and the bar goes red for a line.
Not granted was a faint dot; sealed was purple on purple at 5:1. An ungranted clause reads no write clause in 15px ink with a hatch block beside it. Ceilings print as words in ink. Sensitivity travels on border shape, texture, glyph and word before it uses hue.
A lens announced a re-render and re-rendered nothing. The lens on screen 5 swaps the rows for real, and the withheld ones collapse to a struck counted line.
Ten sections, three of them commentary. A specification wearing console chrome. Five screens and a phone. Everything I have to say sits on a dark page around a frame that is unmistakably a screenshot.

Frames are drawn at true size, 1440×900 and 390×844, and scaled to fit your window. Actual size in the top right turns the scaling off, which is the setting to judge readability in.

The decision I would defend against the other four

Arrivals are queue items and reading one disposes of it. Every other style will treat the conflict queue as a feature bolted onto a browsing surface, which leaves the triage screen empty most weeks and leaves job 1 without a verdict attached to anything it shows; making the arrival the unit gives the console one grammar, a landing screen that is never empty while the store is being written to, and a count in the badge that stays honest because it counts only what needs an answer.

What this style is bad at

Grant composition does not belong in a queue

Screen 5 stops being an inbox the moment it opens. Choosing four clauses across two axes is a spatial problem and a queue is a sequence, so I gave it the whole width and a panel that has no siblings. A policy map will do this better and the owner should compare those two screens directly.

No way to find one specific fact

The queue is ordered by time and there is no search box in it. If the owner remembers writing something in March and wants it now, this console offers scrolling. That is a real gap against a command palette, and the honest patch is the lens control pointed at the console's own credential, which is a poor home for a daily task.

The badge can become a chore in disguise

I designed against it by refusing to ask for a verdict on an arrival, but a number in a circle still pulls. A bad week with thirty conflicts would turn a fifteen-minute ritual into an obligation, and nothing in this design stops that. The measurement in §9 Q6 decides whether the badge is a good idea at all.

Comparison across items is impossible

One item at a time is the whole discipline, so questions of the shape "which namespaces have drifted" or "show me every private row from July" have no surface. Those are real questions and a table would answer them in one glance. §8 rules the browse-all table out of scope, which protects me here more than I deserve.

Two columns of chrome cost 216 pixels forever

The folder column earns its width on the inbox and does nothing at all on the client panel, where it is orientation and no more. A design with a single column would give the reading pane a fifth more room.

It assumes the store is written every week

Arrivals as the landing content works while claude-code-mac writes daily. If the owner stops for a month, this console degrades to the same empty screen I criticised, one layer up. The store panel on screen 7 is the only thing holding that case, and it is thin.

What the owner has to decide, not me

  • The watermark. Unseen and mark all seen put one timestamp in the console backend. If the owner would rather the console keep no state at all, the feature goes and arrivals lose their bold face, which costs the inbox some of its point.
  • The badge counts verdicts, not arrivals. That is a judgement about what deserves to nag. Flip it and the console nags about reading, which is the chore PRD §8 names.
  • Session length on the phone. Job 4's thirty-second clock is a sign-in clock. A session that survives months is a session whose theft survives months, and no layout answers that.
